# The Evprof Script DNA

## Comparative analysis of voice, narrative structure, comedic timing, and visual-storytelling cues

Prepared for storyboard automation prompt and workflow redesign  
Research stage: script analysis only · October 2026

## Executive finding

The defining quality in the two scripts is not simply casual language. They are built as chains of small audience experiences, broken assumptions, visual explanations, and pointed reversals. The narrator keeps speaking to one person and often gives an abstract biological process a concrete personality or object. Those choices create potential visual beats that a storyboard should preserve.

The scripts also use different pacing modes. *Every Weird Body Glitch You Can't Explain* uses short, first-person-feeling incidents with quick punchlines and occasional medical cautions. *The Strangest Things Your Body Does to Keep You Alive* allows longer cause-and-effect demonstrations and escalating stakes. A single repeated scene template would flatten those differences.

## Scope and evidence

### Observed in the two scripts

The analysis directly supports observations about narration structure, language, point of view, recurring rhetorical moves, examples, differences between the two scripts, and imageable moments suggested by their wording.

### Not established by the scripts alone

The scripts do not establish Fred's actual shot selection, character acting, framing, joke visuals, transition vocabulary, or animator handoff conventions. Those require comparison with the matching approved storyboards and videos. Script-writing patterns are not a complete explanation of Evprof's storyboard style.

### Editorial boundary

This is a style and adaptation analysis, not a scientific fact-check. It treats the scripts' medical explanations as source material. A production workflow should preserve qualified wording and flag consequential health claims for editorial review.

## Core storytelling grammar

The following sequence is an observed tendency, not a required structure. Several sections skip or combine steps.

| Beat | What it does | Example from the narration |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drop us into a moment | Begins with a lived experience so the viewer recognizes the situation before technical terms arrive. | “Your phone buzzes in your pocket.” — A, p. 1; “You stand up too fast…” — B, p. 4 |
| Undercut what we assume | Rejects an intuitive explanation, often sharply. | “Your foot didn't nap.” — A, p. 1; “That's completely backward.” — B, p. 1 |
| Explain what is really happening | Moves from outward symptom to internal mechanism; cause and effect matter. | Prediction-machine error for phantom vibration — A, p. 1; platelet/fibrin cascade — B, pp. 3–4 |
| Translate abstraction into everyday language | Uses alarms, machines, plumbing, war, repair crews, or characters with intentions. | “The ringing is your brain patching a hole.” — A, p. 3 |
| Turn it one more time | Ends on irony, consequence, relief, an unsettling implication, or a safety boundary. | “It's actually recovery.” — A, p. 1; clotting can also create dangerous clots — B, p. 4 |

Visual opportunities often occur at the turns between these beats, not automatically at every sentence or at a fixed interval. A scene's emotional point can change even when its subject stays the same.

## Conversational voice

1. **Direct second-person address.** The narrator puts the viewer inside the event: “You sit weird…” and “You stand up too fast…”. This creates a personal subject and an implied on-screen perspective, even when no character is named. (A, pp. 1, 4; B, p. 4)
2. **Contractions and natural speech.** Forms such as “you're,” “it's,” “didn't,” and “here's” make scientific explanation sound spoken rather than read from an encyclopedia. (Throughout A and B)
3. **Fragments as punchlines.** Short bursts such as “Nothing,” “No warning,” and “Then it clears” create timed pauses. The silence after a fragment can matter as much as its literal meaning. (A, pp. 1, 4)
4. **Expectation reversal.** “Not X; actually Y” recurs: a sensation that feels harmful is recovery, discomfort is defense, or involuntary behavior has a purpose. The surprise is structural, not decorative. (A, p. 1; B, pp. 1–3)
5. **A confident, informal narrator.** The voice has opinions: “adorable and completely wrong,” “basically a report card,” and “almost insultingly simple.” It sounds like a person explaining something to a friend. (A, pp. 1–2, 5)
6. **Body systems with agency.** Brains “protest,” nerves “reboot,” bodies “decide,” and defense systems “go on offense.” Internal processes become characters with intentions, creating narrative conflict. (A, pp. 1–2; B, pp. 1, 4)
7. **Specific rather than abstract details.** A phone and pocket, hot stove, hill in a car, ominous text message, concert earplugs, and a cat with raised fur give the narration concrete visual anchors. (A, pp. 1, 3, 5; B, pp. 1, 5)
8. **Escalation and feedback loops.** A viewer notices a symptom, worries, and causes more of the symptom. Muscle twitching and palpitations are told as escalating traps rather than isolated facts. (A, pp. 4, 6)
9. **Humor with a functional job.** A joke usually reframes the mechanism: “Your foot didn't nap” or “your face being honest without permission.” Humor helps make the concept memorable instead of acting as random decoration. (A, pp. 1, 6)
10. **A safety shift in tone.** Some topics deliberately drop the joke to explain when medical attention is needed. The storyboard should notice that shift instead of playing the entire section for laughs. (Chest stitch, palpitations, and brain zaps — A, pp. 2, 5–7)

## Two distinct narrative modes

| Dimension | A · Body Glitch | B · Keep You Alive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Point of entry | First-person bodily sensation: “this happened to me” | Common interpretation: “this seems bad, but…” |
| Voice energy | Quicker, cheekier, more fragments and comedic asides | More sustained and persuasive, with higher stakes |
| Explanation | Short mechanism after a relatable setup | Longer mechanism with competing outcomes and trade-offs |
| Likely visual opportunities* | Visible reactions, mistaken-sensation reveals, comic reversals, then hidden-system cutaways | Systems in action, causal sequences, danger comparisons, and “defense becomes risk” reversals |
| Ending form | Final quip, reassurance, or explicit caution | Survival reframe; some sections show a mechanism's dangerous downside |

*Visual opportunities are hypotheses inferred from narration, not descriptions of existing Evprof animation or storyboard style.

## Visual meaning embedded in the scripts

The scripts are more imageable than a typical textbook explanation because they include concrete actions, conflict and reversal, physical sensations, dramatizable analogies, and changes in mood. These are possibilities to record, not a claim that the first obvious image is the right one.

| Narration cue | What the writing supplies | Visual-storytelling implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Action | A phone buzzes; a hand checks a pocket; someone stands and wobbles; a cut begins closing. | These are physical events in the source. Preserve their order when it matters. |
| Absence or reveal | The phone never buzzed; there are no insects; ringing has no external sound. | The key image may be the contradiction between what a character thinks and what is actually present. |
| Internal mechanism | Nerves misfire; vessels change blood flow; the immune system reacts. | A cutaway or symbolic staging may explain the process, but the channel's usual visual grammar must be learned from storyboards. |
| Metaphor | A prediction machine, alarm, repair crew, eviction notice, or loan that comes due. | These are deliberate writing devices and possible animation concepts, not a requirement to put machines inside every body. |
| Emotional turn | Comfort to unease, fear to relief, reassurance to warning, apparent malfunction to survival strategy. | Follow the change in attitude instead of merely illustrating the nouns. |

## Why a storyboard can feel generic

The following are diagnostic hypotheses to test against Fred's storyboard examples. They are not demonstrated failure modes of the current workflow.

- **Summarizing instead of staging:** “A person experiences tinnitus” omits the silent-room setup, impossible-source mystery, and internal-sound reveal.
- **Flattening rhythm:** Combining the setup, joke, mechanism, and caution in one scene destroys the narrator's successive turns.
- **Defaulting to generic locations:** A fixed room background can contradict a named hot stove, hill, concert, airplane, or other specific setting.
- **Overusing standard science visuals:** A floating brain, organs, arrows, or labels every few lines can replace the story's dramatic idea with a cliché.
- **Treating jokes as decoration:** A random funny expression does not pay off “Your foot didn't nap” or the “loan comes due” metaphor.
- **Ignoring restraint:** Not every explanatory sentence needs a visual gag; medical cautions and invisible sensations may need a tonal shift.

## Proposed prompt and workflow improvements

These proposals prioritize the reasoning pipeline over selecting a more expensive model. They remain provisional until the generated storyboards are compared with Fred's references.

1. **Annotate the narrative, not just the topic.** For each section, extract only the relevant scenario, false assumption, reveal, causal mechanism, analogy, emotional turn, final payoff, safety caveat, and exact script evidence. Keep this as structured intermediate data.
2. **Identify meaningful visual beats.** Mark where the viewer's understanding, location, action, scale, or emotional tone changes. Do not equate sentences with scenes or force identical scene counts across topics.
3. **Retrieve analogous storyboard decisions.** When storyboards are available, retrieve examples by narrative function—such as “mistaken sensation + reveal” or “biological mechanism + reversal”—not only by biomedical keywords.
4. **Generate animator-specific directions.** Translate each beat into the required CSV columns with a concrete subject/action, environment, foreground props, emotional intent, and motion. Respect background-only image requirements if image generation is resumed.
5. **Critique against the source and references.** Check for unsupported visual facts, generic setups, missed ironic turns, bland repeated transitions, difficult-to-animate instructions, and omitted caution beats. Revise weak rows.
6. **Use creator/editor feedback.** Ask which scene decisions are wrong and why, then turn validated corrections into reusable rules. Infer Evprof-specific animation grammar only after studying actual handoffs.

These fields are optional lenses, not a fixed checklist. A section need not contain a reversal, joke, metaphor, or caution.

## Candidate prompt supplement

The source analysis presents this as a starting instruction to test, not as a final Evprof house-style prompt:

> Before writing scenes, analyze the script as a sequence of visual-storytelling beats, not a list of facts. Identify each audience-facing incident, mistaken belief, reveal, mechanism, metaphor, comedic or emotional turn, and safety caveat. Preserve the narrator’s timing and tone. Write scenes that show a specific action or visual change and explain what the animator needs to draw or move; do not simply paraphrase the voiceover. Use concrete settings when the script names them, but do not invent unnecessary characters, locations, scientific assertions or text. Use the supplied approved storyboards to learn scene-depiction conventions. If a stylistic convention cannot be established from those examples, do not pretend it is known.

## Test plan and success criteria

Once matching storyboards are available, score each category from 1 to 5 against a human reference and record one success or failure example per score. Until that comparison exists, “Evprof style match” remains unrated.

| Criterion | Review question |
| --- | --- |
| Script fidelity | Does every visual choice correspond to the source script or a justified staging inference? |
| Narrative-turn fidelity | Are recognition, reversal, mechanism, and punchline or caveat paced distinctly where needed? |
| Scene specificity | Could an animator draw and animate this scene without inventing the key action? |
| Channel-style match | Do the scene ideas and depictions resemble Fred's approved storyboard references beyond topic similarities? |
| Non-generic originality | Does the sequence avoid stock reaction shots, repeated settings, generic science imagery, and redundant labels? |
| Production feasibility | Are backgrounds, hand-drawn characters/props, on-screen text, and motion separated clearly? |

The recommended first comparison is “Phantom Phone Vibration” (short, humor- and reveal-heavy) and “Fainting” (longer, explanation-heavy). Then test two held-out sections that were not used while adjusting prompts. This helps distinguish generalization from memorizing the examples.

## What the storyboards still need to establish

- When animators introduce new scenes versus continue the same shot, including typical shot duration and pacing relative to voiceover.
- How Evprof treats unseen biology: literal internal diagrams, character-level comedy, symbolic metaphors, zoom-ins, cutaways, or combinations.
- The balance between narrator-led humor and animator-added jokes, and which visual jokes are encouraged or excluded.
- The normal specificity and style of “What's happening,” “Hand-drawn characters & props,” and “Transitions/Motion” in Fred's approved CSV.
- How recurring character designs, physical acting, staging, and background locations are chosen across unrelated topics.
- Which health explanations are intentionally simplified by the writer and what scientific/editorial checks the channel applies.

## Conclusion

The raw scripts already provide a strong design brief for each scene: a viewer situation, a contradiction, a cause-and-effect explanation, and an emotional payoff. The first task is to make the workflow notice and preserve those beats. Learning how Evprof actually visualizes them requires the matching storyboards. More model capacity may help, but better structure, reference mapping, and critique are the more defensible first changes.
